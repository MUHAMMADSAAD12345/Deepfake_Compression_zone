Deepfake  detection  has become  increasingly  important  in recent years owing to the widespread  availability  
of deepfake  generation  technologies.  Existing  deepfake  detection  methods  present  two primary  limitations;  
i.e., they are trained  on a specific  type of deepfake  dataset,  which renders  them vulnerable  to unseen  deep- 
fakes, and they regard deepfakes  as a “black box” with  limited  explainability,  making  it difficult  for non-AI  
experts  to understand  and trust the decisions.  Hence,  this article proposes  a novel neurosymbolic  deepfake  
detection  framework  that exploits  the fact that human  emotions  cannot be imitated  easily owing to their 
complex  nature.  We argue that deep fakes typically  exhibit  inter- or intra-modality  inconsistencies  in the 
emotional  expressions  of the person being manipulated.  Thus, the proposed  framework  performs  inter- and 
intra-modality  reasoning  on emotions  extracted  from audio and visual modalities  using a psychological  and 
arousal-valence  model for deepfake  detection.  In addition  to fake detection,  the proposed  framework  pro- 
vides textual  explanations  for its decisions.  The results obtained  using the Presidential  Deepfakes  Dataset  
and World Leaders  Dataset  of real and manipulated  videos demonstrate  the effectiveness  of our approach  in 
detecting  deepfakes  and highlight  the potential  of a neurosymbolic  approach  for expandability.

1 INTRODUCTION  
The advent  of deep learning  has resulted  in the development  of sophisticated  tools for image and 
video manipulation.  These tools can be used for legitimate  purposes,  such as for creating  spe- 
cific effects  in the movie industry.  However,  they can be used by malicious  parties,  e.g., to create 
deepfakes.  Deepfakes  pose a significant  threat to public trust and security,  as they can be used to 
disseminate  wrong  information,  defame  individuals,  or manipulate  public opinion,  specifically  on 
social media [ 1 ]. For instance,  a deepfake  video can be created  to show a political  figure present-  
ing false statements  or engaging  in illegal activities.  An example  in recent  years includes  a deep- 
fake video of Canadian  Prime Minister  Justin Trudeau  circulating  on social media in 2019, which  
showed  Trudeau  discussing  a scandal  involving  the Canadian  government.  Similarly,  a deepfake  
video of former  Vice President  Joe Biden was created  and shared  during  the 2020 election,  when 
Biden appeared  to endorse  President  Trump.  These examples  may result in public outrage  over 
and loss of trust in an individual  or institution;  hence,  the potential  of deepfakes  in influencing  
political  campaigns  or election  outcomes  is concerning.  Deepfakes  are not limited  to politics  [ 2 ]. 
In 2019, a UK-based  energy  company  received  a phone  call from its CEO, which  was in fact a 
deepfake  voice generated  by an AI algorithm,  and was asked to transfer  $243,000  to a Hungarian  
supplier  within  an hour. 
Researchers  are developing  various  deep-learning-based  methods  to combat  the dissemination  
of deep fakes. These methods  focus primarily  on visual artifacts  that occur because  of inconsisten-  
cies in the local texture.  However,  with the rapid advancement  in deepfake  technology,  generating  
highly  realistic  content  has become  possible,  thus rendering  it increasingly  challenging  to differen-  
tiate between  real and fake media using existing  techniques.  In other methods,  biometric  signals  
such as heartbeat  rate [ 3 ], emotions  [ 4 ], and eye-blinking  [ 5 ] patterns  are used, where  the tar- 
geted facial motion  of an individual  is prioritized,  thus limiting  generalization  to new identities.  
Furthermore,  most deepfake  detection  techniques  rely on black-box  models  that cannot  interpret  
or explain  the reason  that a video is classified  as fake. Hence,  they do not garner  the trust of the 
audience.  Some explainable  deepfake  detection  approaches  have been reported  in the literature;  
however,  most of them perform  decisions  based on typical  attention  mechanisms  such as Grad- 
CAM [ 6 ], that can only highlight  a specific  region  via the heatmaps  of an image or video. Despite  
the significant  progress  in deepfake  detection,  several  research  questions  are yet to be addressed,  
as follows:  
(1) How can logical  reasoning  be effectively  incorporated  across  multiple  modalities  to en- 
hance deepfake  detection?  
(2) What psychological  knowledge  is necessary  to improve  the generalizability  of deepfake  
detection  techniques?  
(3) How can explainable  approaches  be developed  to make deepfake  detection  accessible  to 
non-AI  experts?  
To answer  these questions,  this article  proposes  a multimodal  neurosymbolic  approach  for deep- 
fake detection.  We argue that human  emotion  psychology  is one of the most prominent  biological  
signals  for deepfake  detection  due to its complex  nature,  which  cannot  be perfectly  replicated  
by generative  algorithms.  Therefore,  we studied  the specific  probabilities  of transitions  from one

emotional  state to another  that results  in consistent  emotional  stability  in individuals.  Our aim is 
to detect irregular  transitions  (with low probability)  among  various  emotions  in a single modal-  
ity. However,  we observed  a natural  connection  between  visual and aural emotions  at a specified  
timestamp,  which  must be consistent  in most cases. Therefore,  the proposed  method  integrates  
multimodal  and neurosymbolic  approaches  to perform  human-understandable  reasoning  for deep- 
fake detection.  According  to Henry  Kautz [ 7 ], the proposed  framework  can be categorized  into a 
Type 2 (i.e., Symbolic[Neuro])  architecture,  where  neural  modules  are used as subroutine  within  
a symbolic  problem  solver.  In summary,  the symbolic  and neural  components  are loosely  coupled.  
Specifically,  the proposed  framework  first extracts  emotions  from visual and aural modalities  via 
deep models,  which  is the neural  component.  Subsequently,  inter- and intra-modality  reasoning  
is performed  in the symbolic  module  to determine  the fakeness  of a specified  video. Unlike  ex- 
isting explainable  methods  that use Grad-CAM  [ 6 ] to highlight  the portion  of a frame to be fo- 
cused,  the proposed  neurosymbolic  approach  can provide  interpretable  reasoning  details  that can 
be converted  to textual  explanations  for non-AI  experts.  The main contributions  of this study are 
summarized  as follows:  
(1) A novel biological  signal (which  demonstrates  consistency  between  emotion  transitions)  
is proposed  for deepfake  detection.  The proposed  framework  performs  inter- and intra- 
modality  reasoning  to detect inconsistencies  in emotional  transitions  in visual and aural 
modalities.  
(2) To the best of our knowledge,  this study is the first to utilize  external  knowledge  (i.e., psy- 
chological  and arousal-valence  dimensions)  for deepfake  detection  using a neurosymbolic  
approach,  which  renders  the overall  framework  more interpretable.  
(3) The proposed  framework  provides  textual  explanations  (domain  specific)  with localization  
of the timestamp  along with fakeness  detection,  which  renders  it more reliable  and un- 
derstandable  for explaining  decisions  compared  with state-of-the-art  deepfake  detection  
methods.  
The remainder  of the article  is organized  as follows:  Section  2 presents  the literature  on deepfake  
detection  algorithms.  Section  3 presents  the technical  details  pertaining  to the proposed  deepfake  
detection  algorithm.  The experimental  settings  and results  are discussed  in Section  4 . Finally,  the 
findings  of the study and future research  directions  are presented  in Section  5 . 
2 LITERATURE  REVIEW  
The literature  on deepfake  detection  can be broadly  classified  into two categories:  Section  2.1 
artifact-based  methods  and Section  2.2 biological-signal-based  methods.  Artifact-based  methods  
focus primarily  on detecting  blending  traces that occur during  deepfake  generation,  whereas  
biological-signal-based  methods  focus on individual  behaviors  and various  psychological  cues. 
Both categories  consider  spatial  and temporal  features  along with single or multimodal  data. The 
following  subsections  discuss  each category  in detail. 
2.1 Artifact-based  Methods  
One of the most challenging  aspects  of deepfake  generation  algorithms  is blending  the source  
face with the target with minimum  blending  artifacts.  To detect such artifacts  for deepfake  detec- 
tion, Durall  et al. [ 8 ] performed  a discrete  Fourier  transform  with an azimuthal  average  to analyze  
these artifacts  in the frequency  domain  and make them more visible  in the detection  algorithm.  
Subsequently,  several  classification  algorithms,  such as the SVM, LR, and K-mean,  were trained  to 
classify  deepfakes.  Li and Lyu [ 31 ] combined  VGG16,  ResNet50,  ResNet101,  and ResNet152  to an- 
alyze artifacts  in low-resolution  deepfakes  wrapped  to fit the target face. This method  was further

improved  in another  study [ 9 ] by adding  a pooling  layer, which  resulted  in better performance  
in detecting  variations  in the resolution  of the target and deepfake  media.  Similarly,  Nirkin  et al. 
[ 10 ] first segmented  a test image into face and hair/context  and then generated  three different  
encodings.  Subsequently,  these encodings  were used in a decoder  for classification,  where  the en- 
coding  of the source  was concatenated  with the difference  between  the face-only  and hair/context  
encodings.  
Masi et al. [ 11 ] proposed  a two-stream  framework  consisting  of bidirectional  LSTM,  with em- 
phasis  on residual  errors for deepfake  detection.  One stream  uses frequency  to reduce  the errors,  
whereas  the other stream  fine-tunes  the original  information  in the color domain.  Recently,  Zhao 
et al. [ 12 ] proposed  a transformer-based  self-attention  and self-subtracting  mechanism  for spatial  
artifact  and temporal  inconsistency  detection.  They mainly  focused  on investigating  the supported  
learning  mechanism  by analyzing  the proposed  framework  using the Grad-CAM  [ 6 ]. Typical  Deep- 
Fake videos  often indicate  inconsistencies  in certain  facial features  across  frames,  which  can be 
detected  via appearance-level  analysis.  To detect such inconsistencies,  Ge et al. [ 13 ] proposed  a 
predictive  representative  learning  approach  that combines  a CNN encoder,  a ConvGRU-based  ag- 
gregator,  and a single-layer  binary  classifier  to capture  semantic  changes  specific  to deepfakes.  
Some researchers  have addressed  deepfake  detection  as an anomaly  detection  problem.  For exam- 
ple, Sun et al. [ 14 ] analyzed  facial region  trajectories  for deepfake  detection.  In their method,  the 
face region  displacement  was first calculated;  subsequently,  a trajectory  series was constructed,  
which  was then analyzed  for deepfake  detection.  
2.2 Biological-signal-based  Methods  
Unlike  GANs,  which  use sets of latent spaces  for deepfake  generation,  recent  diffusion  probabilistic  
models  have achieved  high-quality  deepfake  generation  with almost  no residual  artifact  traces.  
Thus, biological  signals  such as heart rate, respiration,  and emotions  are considered  better options  
for high-quality  deepfake  detection  where  visual artifacts  are less apparent.  Various  techniques  
have been described  in the literature  that utilize  heart rate analysis  to detect deepfakes  based on 
biological  signals  [ 15 –17 ]. The challenge  with these approaches  is extracting  accurate  heart rates 
from visual signals.  For instance,  Jin et al. [ 18 ] used a multidimensional  feature  space from the 
RGB, Y U V, and chromatic  dimensions  to extract  heart rate from a visual modality.  Subsequently,  
these multidimensional  features  were used to classify  deep fakes using a CNN as a binary  classifier.  
Similarly,  Fernandes  et al. [ 19 ] proposed  a neural  ordinary  differential  equation-based  model for 
detecting  heart rate changes  over time. They extracted  the heart rate by measuring  skin color 
variations,  optical  intensities  from the forehead,  and temporal  changes  in color. 
In addition  to the heart rate, several  individual  behavior-based  approaches  have been reported  
for deepfake  detection  [ 20 ]. For example,  Agarwal  and Farid [ 21 ] investigated  the dynamics  of 
synchrony  between  audio and visual modalities.  They argued  that the movement  of the mandible  
(lower  jaw) caused  changes  in the shape of the ear and ear canal during  talking.  They analyzed  
the correlation  between  the corresponding  facial landmarks  and speech  signals.  Recently,  Agarwal  
et al. [ 22 ] presented  a similar  approach,  with emphasis  on the synchronization  between  spoken  
words and facial movements  in a video. They used pretrained  facial landmark  detection  and a text- 
based model to extract  facial movements  and spoken  words and learned  person-specific  biometric  
patterns.  
Additionally,  the potential  of emotions  in deepfake  detection  has been investigated.  For exam- 
ple, Mazaheri  and Roy-Chowdhury  [ 23 ] customized  a pretrained  facial expression  recognition  
( FER ) model for deepfake  detection  with the additional  application  of localizing  the section  of 
the face that is involved  in false expressions  using Grad-CAM  [ 6 ]. Mittal et al. [ 4 ] proposed  a 
multimodal  deepfake  detection  model that uses facial expression  analysis  and speech  emotion

channels,  which  are likely to occur in deepfake  videos.  Similarly,  Hosler  et al. [ 24 ] focused  on the 
inconsistencies  between  aural and visual emotions  for deepfake  detection.  In this method,  the low- 
level descriptors  of a subject’s  speech  and face are first extracted  to present  the subject’s  emotions  
over time; subsequently,  a recurrent  neural  network  is trained  on deepfake  videos.  These methods  
are closely  related  to the proposed  method  in terms of emotion  analysis  from multiple  modalities.  
However,  our study differs  from [ 4 , 23 , 24 ] in two respects:  we consider  both inter- and intra- 
modality  inconsistencies  in emotions  and perform  reasoning  based on psychological  knowledge  
instead  of deciding  the fakeness  of a video based on the error score. 
3 PROPOSED  METHODOLOGY  
This section  discusses  the technical  details  of the proposed  deepfake  detection  framework.  The 
overall  framework  is shown  in Figure  1 . First, the input video is preprocessed  and forwarded  to 
the SER and FER models.  Second,  the predicted  emotions  are symbolized  in the reasoning  module,  
where  intra- and inter-modality  reasoning  is performed  to determine  the fakeness  of a given video. 
Finally,  the framework  predicts  the fakeness  of the video and provides  a short textual  explanation  
for interpretability.  Each step in the proposed  framework  is discussed  in the following  subsections.  
3.1 Problem  Formulation  
Consider  V and A as the visual and aural modalities  of the input video I to be tested for fakeness,  
respectively,  whereas  s i and f i are the audio segments  and video frames  of the synchronized  times-  
tamps that are preprocessed  and forwarded  to SER and FER models,  respectively.  The predicted  
emotions  from V and A at timestamp  i are denoted  as f i (E) and s i (E) , respectively.  Our goal is to 
determine  the fakeness  of an input video with an explanation  for which  inter- and intra-modality  
symbolic  reasoning  is performed  to detect fake emotion  transitions  based on the psychological  
knowledge  base KB and arousal-valence  dimensions  AVD . The proposed  framework  can identify  
deepfake  videos  along with textual  explanations  that localize  the timestamp  and identify  fake- 
ness in inter- or intra-modalities.  Table 1 presents  the symbols  and their descriptions.  Algorithm  
1 explains  the overall  flow of the proposed  framework.

3.3 Multimodal  Emotion  Detection  
Emotion  is a prominent  yet complex  biological  signal for the generation  of deepfakes.  Therefore,  
the preprocessed  signals  were forwarded  to detect emotions  over time from both the V and A of 
the input video. Facial landmarks,  color, texture,  and various  acoustic  properties  such as pitch, 
intensity,  and spectral  attributes  were considered.  To extract  emotions  from both modalities,  we 
employed  pretrained  FER and SER models  that classified  the input signals  into seven basic emo- 
tions: angry,  disgust,  fear, happy,  sad, surprise,  and neutral.  
3.3.1 Facial Emotion  Recognition  (FER). FER involves  the identification  and extraction  of vari- 
ous facial landmarks  such as the eyes, nose, mouth,  and eyebrows.  Subsequently,  these landmarks  
are used to compute  the movement  of each landmark,  thus providing  valuable  information  regard-  
ing the emotional  state of the individual.  In the proposed  framework,  we adopted  an FER model 
[ 26 ] based on the ResNet50  architecture.  This model comprised  multiple  convolutional  layers with 
ReLU activation,  followed  by max pooling  and batch normalization.  The output  of the CNN was 
fed to a fully connected  layer, which  yielded  the final classification  via a softmax  classifier.  We 
trained  this model on the FER2013  dataset  [ 27 ] at a learning  rate of 0.001 and a batch size of 128 
for 50 epochs.  Further  technical  details  are available  in an online  repository  [ 26 ].

3.3.2 Speech  Emotion  Recognition  (SER). To extract  speech  emotions  from the preprocessed  
aural signals,  we fine-tuned  one of the existing  implementations  of the SER repository  based on 
our problem.  We retrained  this model on seven basic emotions  from the RAVDESS  dataset  [ 28 ] 
by excluding  the “clam” class.  First, the MFCC,  Chroma,  and MEL spectrograms  were extracted;  
subsequently,  they were fed into the MLP classifier.  The best performance  was achieved  when the

and 20% test set. Further  technical  details  are available  in [ 29 ]. 
3.4 Symbolic  Reasoning  Module  
We argue that emotions  are psychological  phenomena  that are not perfectly  emulated  during  deep- 
fake generation  due to their complex  nature.  Therefore,  the proposed  symbolic  reasoning  module  
performs  reasoning  regarding  the extracted  emotions  in two manners:  (1) intra-modality  reason-  
ing, in which  the inconsistency  in detected  emotions  focuses  on each modality  over time, and 
(2) inter-modality  reasoning,  which  focuses  on inconsistencies  in the valence-arousal  space be- 
tween aural and visual modalities.  Intra- and inter-modality  reasoning  are based on the psycho-  
logical  knowledge  base KB for emotional  state transition  and the valence-arousal  distributions  
VAD , respectively.  To perform  logical  reasoning,  we first convert  the detected  aural and visual 
emotions  into symbols  over time, as shown  in Equations  ( 1 ) and ( 2 ): 
V E = [ f i ( E ) , f i+1 ( E ) , f i+2 ( E ) . . . f i+n ( E ) ] (1) 
AE = [ s i ( E ) , s i+1 ( E ) , s i+2 ( E ) ... s i+n ( E ) ] , (2) 
where  VE and AE are the sets of emotions  detected  from V and A modalities  over time, respectively.

3.4.2 Inter-modality  Reasoning.  The visual and aural modalities  for deepfake  detection  have 
been compared  previously  [ 4 , 31 ]. However,  these methods  rely on the final loss value to de-

well-established  emotion  models  [ 32 ] (i.e., arousal-valence  dimensions),  which  is typically  used 
in psychology,  and captures  the emotional  affect through  valence  and the intensity  of the emo-

detects  the happy  emotion  and the visual modality  detects  the surprise  emotion,  then the result

4 EXPERIMENTAL  EVALUATION  
This section  presents  the details  of the experimental  setup, optimal  settings  for the proposed  
method,  dataset  description,  and performance  analysis  performed  to demonstrate  the effective-  
ness of the proposed  neurosymbolic  approach  for DFD. 
4.1 Experimental  Setup  and Dataset  
To conduct  the experiments,  we converted  the input videos  into sets of cropped  faces from the 
visual modality  and the corresponding  0.5 s audio segments  from the aural modality.  The FER 
and SER models  were trained  on a system  configured  with a GPU with 24 GB of memory,  64 
GB of RAM, and CUDA  11.0. The implementation  was performed  using the Tensorflow  2.0.5 and 
Keras 2.0.0 frameworks  in Python  3.7, in the Anaconda  environment.  The proposed  method  was 
evaluated  on the Presidential  Deepfakes  Dataset  (PDD)  [ 33 ], which  contains  32 video clips 
of the two most recent  US presidents,  Donald  Trump  and Joseph  Biden.  Half of the videos  were 
deepfake  and were created  using various  deep learning  techniques,  such as Wav2Lip  and fake au- 
dio. The complete  dataset  contained  20,425  frames,  which  were 11 min and 26 s in length.  Unlike  
other methods  that use a standard  ratio of datasets  for training  and testing,  we used the overall  
dataset  presented  in [ 33 ]. Figure  3 (a) shows  samples  from the PDD. Additionally,  we evaluated  the 
proposed  framework  on the World  Leaders  Dataset  (WLD)  [ 34 ], which  comprised  videos  featur-  
ing FaceSwap-manipulated  images  of various  political  leaders,  including  Obama,  Hillary  Clinton,  
Elizabeth  Warren,  and Bernie  Sanders.  Actual  videos  were obtained  from YouTube,  and the ma- 
nipulated  samples  were created  using swapped  faces of the leaders’  comedic  impersonators.  In 
this dataset,  we used only actual (both visual and aural real) and deepfake  (both visual and aural 
manipulated)  samples  and discarded  the imposter  class. Figure  3 (b) shows  some sample  images  
from the WLD.

4.7 Discussion  on Explainability  and Generalizability  
The development  of explainable  and generalizable  methods  is crucial  for enhancing  the trust- 
worthiness  and reliability  of machine  learning  models.  Recently,  the field of explainable  AI has 
advanced  significantly  owing  to the emergence  of new techniques  and methodologies  to improve  
the interpretability  and comprehensibility  of machine  learning  models.  However,  they are pri- 
marily  intended  for general  purposes  and focus on explaining  the learning  of deep models  (i.e., 
Grad-CAM  [ 6 ], LRP [ 37 ], and PRM [ 38 ]), which  are not directly  beneficial  to non-AI  experts.  
However,  obtaining  a generalized  solution  for all types of deepfakes  is challenging  because  of the 
continuous  evolution  of deepfake  generation  algorithms.  Thus, in this study, we aim to improve  
the explainability  and generalizability  of deepfake  detection  algorithms.  One of the main advan-  
tages of the proposed  framework  is its ability  to provide  clear and concise  explanations  regarding  
the decisions  made by the model using psychological  knowledge  of emotions.  Our framework  
is based on a novel biological  signal that combines  deep neural  networks  with knowledge-based  
assisted  symbolic  reasoning,  thus enabling  both accurate  predictions  and interpretable  explana-  
tions. Symbolic  reasoning  serves  as an intuitive  approach  for representing  the decision-making  
process  of a framework,  thus allowing  it to be easily understood  and interpreted.  An example  that 
explains  real and fake videos  is illustrated  in Figure  6 , which  allows  non-AI  experts  to easily under-  
stand the rationale  behind  the proposed  framework’s  predictions.  The Indx and Prob values  from 
Equations  ( 7 ) and ( 8 ) are used to visualize  Figure  6 , which  indicate  the timestamp  at which  the fake 
emotion  transition  and mismatch  in aural and visual emotions  occurred.  For instance,  Figure  6 (a) 
shows  an example  of a real video classified  as real, with extremely  few fake emotion  transitions  
and mismatched  emotions  (red highlighted  portions).  By contrast,  a fake video classified  as fake 
contains  more fake emotion  transitions  and mismatched  emotions  in both modalities,  with more 
red highlighted  portions.  In addition  to explainability,  the proposed  framework  exhibits  a high 
degree  of generalizability  owing  to the use of common  knowledge  and is independent  of training  
on deepfake  data. Our strategy  enables  the model to leverage  knowledge  learned  from psychol-  
ogy and emotions  to deepen  fake domains.  Thus, transferring  common  knowledge  across  domains  
renders  the proposed  framework  more generalizable.

5 CONCLUSION  
In this study, a novel framework  that combines  psychological  knowledge  and symbolic  reasoning  
to address  the deepfake  detection  was proposed.  The aim of this study was to improve  the explain-  
ability  and generalizability  of deepfake  detection  methods.  Hence,  we introduced  a novel biological  
signal (which  demonstrates  consistency  between  emotion  transitions)  based on a psychological  
analysis  of intra-modality  reasoning,  along with arousal-valence  dimensions  for inter-modality  
reasoning.  The proposed  framework  utilizes  psychological  knowledge,  which  can be explained  in 
terms of emotional  consistency.  Furthermore,  no deepfake  dataset  is required  for training,  which  
renders  the proposed  framework  more generalizable  owing  to the underlying  knowledge  learned  
from psychology  and emotions  in deepfake  domains.  Thus, transferring  common  knowledge  
across  domains  renders  the proposed  framework  more generalizable.  We demonstrated  that the 
proposed  framework  correctly  classifies  videos  misclassified  by the deep learning  method,  which  
confirmed  the effectiveness  of our approach  in detecting  deepfakes  and highlighted  the potential  
of a neurosymbolic  approach  for achieving  explainability.  The proposed  framework  demonstrated  
remarkable  performance  on the PDD, with an accuracy  of 93.7%. Similarly,  it achieved  an overall  
accuracy  of 75.34%  on the WLD. In the future,  we plan to introduce  artifact  cues along with 
biological  signals  to further  improve  performance  over a diverse  range of deepfake  datasets.
